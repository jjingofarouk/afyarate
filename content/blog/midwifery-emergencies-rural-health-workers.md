At a well-staffed regional hospital, an obstetric emergency means calling in an obstetrician, an anaesthetist, and a full theatre team within minutes. At a rural Health Centre III or IV in Uganda, it often means one midwife, a dim delivery room, and a decision about whether the mother can survive the journey to the next referral point. That gap is exactly why the emergencies below deserve to be second nature, not something you look up when they are already happening in front of you.

This is not a replacement for your clinical training or your facility's protocols. It is a working refresher on recognition and first response, the kind of thing worth revisiting before every busy delivery season.

## Postpartum haemorrhage

This remains one of the leading causes of maternal death in Uganda, and it can escalate in minutes. The moment bleeding after delivery looks heavier than normal, act rather than watch and wait.

Start with uterine massage and check that the uterus is well contracted. Give oxytocin promptly if it has not already been given as part of active management of the third stage. Check for retained placental fragments and for tears that need repair. If bleeding continues, get a second set of hands, start an IV line if you have not already, and begin preparing for referral before the mother becomes unstable, not after. Waiting until blood pressure drops to call for transport costs precious time you will not get back.

Keep a running mental count of estimated blood loss rather than a vague impression. It changes how urgently you act.

## Pre-eclampsia and eclampsia

A woman arriving in labour with a headache, visual disturbance, or epigastric pain deserves a blood pressure check before anything else, even if she says she is "just tired." Severe pre-eclampsia can progress to convulsions with very little warning.

If a seizure occurs, protect the airway, position her on her left side, and give magnesium sulphate according to your facility's protocol if it is available and you are trained to administer it. Magnesium sulphate is the single most important drug in preventing a second seizure, and facilities that stock it save lives more reliably than those that don't. If you are unsure of your centre's current stock, that is worth checking today, not during the emergency itself.

## Obstructed labour

Slow progress that has genuinely stalled, not just labour that is taking a normal long time, is one of the most dangerous situations a rural midwife will face, because the safe window for referral closes as the labour continues. Watch the partograph honestly. When the line crosses the action line, that is not a suggestion, it is a signal to begin referral arrangements immediately, not after another hour of watching.

A prolonged second stage, a mother who is exhausted, or signs of fetal distress on top of poor progress all shorten your window further. Obstructed labour left too long risks uterine rupture, fistula, and stillbirth, outcomes that are almost always preventable with timely referral.

## Cord prolapse

This is one of the true immediate emergencies in obstetrics. If the cord is visible or felt below the presenting part, every second matters. Get the mother into a knee-chest or exaggerated Sims position immediately, manually elevate the presenting part off the cord with a gloved hand and keep it there, and arrange the fastest possible transfer for emergency delivery, usually caesarean section. Do not remove your hand until the mother is on the operating table. This is a situation where you may need to travel in the vehicle maintaining that pressure yourself.

## Shoulder dystocia

When the head delivers but the shoulders will not follow, resist the urge to pull harder. Call for help immediately, and work through a structured sequence: hyperflex the mother's legs firmly onto her abdomen, apply suprapubic pressure, and attempt internal rotational manoeuvres if you are trained in them. Document the time the head delivered, since this matters for the baby's outcome assessment afterward. Panic and excessive traction are what cause the nerve injuries associated with this emergency, a calm, sequential approach is what prevents them.

## Newborn who does not breathe

Have your resuscitation equipment checked and within arm's reach before every single delivery, not fetched after the baby fails to cry. Dry the baby, stimulate, and clear the airway if needed within the first minute. If the baby is not breathing adequately, begin bag and mask ventilation without delay. The first minute after birth is the window that determines outcome far more than anything done afterward, and it is a window that is entirely within a midwife's control if the equipment is ready in advance.

## The thread running through all of these

Every one of these emergencies shares the same lesson: the outcome is decided less by dramatic heroics and more by whether you recognised the situation early, acted on your training without hesitation, and started the referral conversation before you were forced to. Working alone at a rural facility does not mean working without a plan. Know your nearest functional referral point, know how long transport realistically takes, and rehearse these scenarios in your head before your next shift, not during it.
