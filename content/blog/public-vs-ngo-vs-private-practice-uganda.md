Almost every Ugandan health worker eventually faces this decision, sometimes more than once in a career: stay in the government system, move into the NGO and donor-funded project world, or go private. Each path has genuinely different trade-offs, and the right answer depends less on which pays more this month and more on what you are optimising for over the next ten years. Here is an honest look at how the three actually compare.

## Government service

Working within the public health system, whether at a district hospital, regional referral hospital, or health centre, is still how the majority of Uganda's health workforce is employed. The appeal is structural rather than glamorous: a permanent pensionable position, a scheme of service with defined promotion steps, and job security that does not depend on a donor renewing a grant.

Pay in government service tends to be the most stable but also the most modest compared to the other two paths, and it moves slowly, tied to public sector pay scales and government budget cycles rather than market rates. What government service offers instead is predictability. Your salary lands the same way every month, you accumulate NSSF and pension benefits over a full career, and your position does not disappear because a three year project grant ended.

Government postings, particularly to rural and hard to reach facilities, also come with hardship allowances in many cases, though these have long been a subject of debate over whether they are enough to offset the real difficulty of working in an under-resourced, understaffed rural facility. Career progression exists but tends to be slower and more seniority-based than merit alone.

## NGO and donor-funded project work

This is where a large share of Uganda's more competitive salaries in health actually sit, particularly for roles tied to HIV, TB, malaria, maternal health, and public health research programmes funded by international donors. Pay is usually noticeably higher than equivalent government roles, sometimes substantially so, and NGO employers often offer additional benefits like better health insurance, transport allowances, or training opportunities abroad.

The trade-off is precarity. Most NGO and project positions are tied directly to grant cycles, typically running one to five years, and renewal is never guaranteed. It is common, and often expected, for skilled health workers to move between two or three different projects over the course of a decade as funding shifts. This path rewards people who are comfortable with some uncertainty and proactive about networking and watching for the next opportunity before the current contract ends, rather than assuming the role will simply continue.

NGO work also tends to build a different kind of resume, strong in specific technical areas like research, data, or a particular disease programme, which can be an advantage when applying internationally, but sometimes narrower in the breadth of general clinical exposure compared to a busy government hospital rotation.

## Private practice and private facilities

Private hospitals, clinics, and pharmacies range enormously, from small single-owner clinics to larger private hospital chains, and pay and conditions vary just as widely. At the higher end, particularly in Kampala and other major towns, private facility salaries can be competitive with or exceed both government and some NGO roles, especially for specialists and experienced practitioners who can negotiate directly.

The catch is that private sector job security depends entirely on the individual employer's stability, there is no pension scheme equivalent to government service unless the employer specifically provides one, and smaller private facilities in particular can be inconsistent about paying salaries on time. It is worth asking directly, before accepting an offer, how consistently the facility has paid staff historically, and speaking to current or former employees if you can.

Private practice is also where entrepreneurial paths open up most directly, whether that is eventually opening your own clinic, pharmacy, or diagnostic facility once you have the required licensing and experience. For registered health workers with the right qualifications and some capital, this is a genuine route to significantly higher long-term earning potential, though it comes with real business risk that employed positions do not carry.

## So which one should you choose

Most experienced Ugandan health workers do not stay in one lane their entire career, and that is not a failure of commitment, it is often the smartest strategy available. A common and reasonable pattern looks like this: start in government service or a hospital rotation to build broad clinical experience and complete any required bonding period, move into NGO or project work once you have marketable experience to build savings and specialised skills faster, and consider private practice or a private facility role once you have enough experience, reputation, and ideally capital to negotiate well or start your own venture.

[IMAGE: A confident health worker reviewing multiple job offers or contracts at a desk]

If job security and a predictable long-term pension matter most to you right now, government service remains the strongest option. If maximising current income and building specialised technical experience matters more, and you can tolerate contract uncertainty, NGO work is usually the better trade. If you are ready to take on more risk in exchange for higher ceiling earnings and more control over your working life, private practice is where that becomes possible.

None of these choices are permanent. Understanding what each path is actually optimising for, rather than just comparing this month's take-home pay, is what makes the decision easier the next time it comes around.
