If you are due to start your medical internship in Uganda this year, or you know someone who is, the last few months have probably left you confused about what you're actually going to be paid and when you're supposed to report. You are not alone. The 2026/2027 internship intake has been one of the most turbulent in recent memory, and the situation changed more than once within a matter of weeks. Here is what actually happened, where things stand now, and what it means for you.

## Why the internship year exists in the first place

Every doctor, dentist, pharmacist, and graduate nurse or midwife trained in Uganda has to complete a supervised one-year internship before the relevant professional council (UMDPC for doctors and dentists, the Pharmacy Council, or UNMC for nursing and midwifery) will grant a full practising licence. It is not optional and it is not just a formality. You are attached to a recognised internship centre, usually a regional referral hospital or a large general hospital, and you rotate through major departments under supervision. Only after completing it and being signed off can you register as a fully licensed, independently practising professional.

Because internship centres are limited and the process is centrally coordinated by the Ministry of Health, thousands of new graduates are deployed at once, on a schedule the Ministry sets each year. That centralised structure is exactly why a single policy decision in Kampala can affect the income and plans of thousands of young health workers overnight.

## What changed in 2026

In June 2026, the Ministry of Health signalled a major shift: intern allowances would be phased out, on the reasoning that the internship year should be treated as a continuation of training rather than a form of paid employment. For interns who had budgeted around a monthly stipend, many of them supporting themselves or family members on it, this was a serious blow. It also came at a time when reports of unpaid interns and posting delays were already straining relationships between trainee doctors and hospital administrations, with some interns threatening strike action over the uncertainty.

The backlash was immediate and vocal. Medical associations and intern representatives pushed back publicly, arguing that a full clinical year, often involving long hours, night calls, and real patient responsibility, is materially different from classroom-based training and deserves compensation.

[IMAGE: Uganda Ministry of Health signage or building exterior, official government context]

By mid-August 2026, the government reversed course. Health Minister Dr Chris Baryomunsi announced that the monthly stipend of one million Ugandan shillings would be maintained for all medical interns, including privately sponsored trainees who had specifically been at risk of losing support under the earlier proposal. Alongside that reversal, the Ministry said it had expanded the number of recognised internship centres to 77 and was deploying about 2,490 interns across doctors, pharmacists, dentists, graduate nurses, and midwives for the new intake. The original reporting deadline of August 14 was pushed back to August 21, 2026, giving affected interns extra time to sort out placement logistics after weeks of uncertainty.

## What this means if you are an incoming or current intern

The immediate financial question has, for now, been settled in interns' favour: the one million shilling monthly stipend stands. But the speed at which policy moved this year, from phase-out, to public standoff, to reversal, is a reminder that this is not a settled, permanent arrangement. A few practical takeaways:

**Confirm your placement directly with your assigned centre, not just word of mouth.** With 77 centres now in the rotation and a compressed reporting window, mix-ups are more likely than usual this cycle. A phone call or visit to the HR or medical superintendent's office at your assigned hospital is worth the trip.

**Keep your own paperwork.** Internship completion letters, attendance records, and any correspondence about your allowance are documents you may need later, both for council registration and if disputes about pay or posting arise. Don't rely on a hospital's administrative memory a year from now.

**Join or stay close to your professional association.** Whether that's a medical students' and interns' association or your specific cadre's body, collective representation is what actually moved the needle in this year's standoff. Individual interns had very little leverage on their own.

**Watch official channels, not rumour.** Because this story moved fast and unevenly across different hospitals, misinformation spread quickly in the middle of it. The Ministry of Health's own statements, and your professional council's communications, are the sources to check before making decisions based on something you heard secondhand.

## The bigger picture

Uganda produces more health graduates each year than its internship and employment system can comfortably absorb without friction, and the internship allowance question is really a symptom of a wider strain: a health workforce that is growing faster than the budget lines built to support it. That tension is unlikely to disappear after this year's reversal. If anything, the willingness of interns to organise and push back publicly this year suggests future policy changes in this space will be watched, and contested, closely.

If you are weighing where to apply for internship, when your placement letter is likely to arrive, or what your rights are around allowances, treat this year's events as a case study rather than a one-off. The system responded to pressure. It can shift again, in either direction, and staying informed through your professional body is the best protection you have.
