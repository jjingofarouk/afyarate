Most CVs from Ugandan health workers are rejected before anyone reads past the first page, not because the applicant is unqualified, but because the document itself makes it hard for a busy HR officer to quickly confirm they are. When a hospital or NGO is sorting through eighty applications for one clinical officer position, a confusing or poorly organised CV gets set aside within seconds, regardless of how strong the candidate actually is. Here is how to fix that.

## Start with what Ugandan employers actually expect to see

Unlike CV conventions in some other countries, it is standard practice in Uganda to include a passport-style photo on your CV, along with full contact details, your nationality, and sometimes your marital status and religion, though the last two are increasingly optional and some applicants choose to leave them out. Check the job advert itself, some organisations, particularly international NGOs, explicitly ask you not to include a photo to reduce bias in screening. When in doubt and no instruction is given, including a professional, recent photo remains the safer default for the Ugandan job market specifically.

Your full names should match your certificates and licence exactly. A mismatch between what's on your CV, your degree certificate, and your professional council registration is one of the most common reasons a shortlist gets delayed while HR tries to verify who you actually are.

## Structure it so the reader finds what they need in ten seconds

A hiring panel scanning your CV is looking for three things almost immediately: are you qualified for this specific role, are you currently licensed to practise, and where have you worked before. Structure your CV so those answers are impossible to miss.

Lead with your personal details and a short professional summary, three or four lines maximum, stating your profession, years of experience, and area of focus. Follow immediately with your professional qualifications and licence or registration status, including your council registration number if you have one. Many applicants bury this on page two. It should be near the top, because it is often the first thing that determines whether you even meet the minimum requirement for the role.

After that, list your work experience in reverse chronological order, most recent first, with clear dates and a few specific bullet points per role describing what you actually did, not a vague job description copied from the original posting. "Managed antenatal care for an average of 40 patients weekly at a Health Centre IV, including identifying and referring high-risk pregnancies" tells a panel far more than "responsible for antenatal care."

## Common mistakes that get Ugandan health worker CVs rejected

**Listing every training and workshop you have ever attended.** A five page CV dense with every single CPD workshop since your internship does not read as impressive, it reads as unfiltered. Include your most relevant and most recent trainings, particularly anything specific to the role you're applying for, and summarise the rest as "additional short courses available on request."

**Vague or missing dates.** Gaps in your work history are not automatically disqualifying, but an unexplained gap combined with vague or missing dates makes a panel assume the worst. If you took time off, briefly say why, further study, family responsibilities, it is normal and rarely counts against you when stated plainly.

**Attaching everything instead of what was asked for.** If the advert asks for a CV, application letter, and copies of academic transcripts, send exactly that. Attaching every certificate you own, scanned at inconsistent quality, in a single ten megabyte email, is more likely to frustrate an HR officer than impress them. Follow the instructions in the advert precisely, this is itself a small test of whether you can follow instructions on the job.

**No application letter, or a generic one reused for every job.** A short, specific cover letter that names the organisation and the exact role, and briefly says why you are a strong fit, still matters in Uganda's hiring culture more than it might elsewhere. A generic letter that could apply to any employer signals low effort.

## Before you hit send

Have someone else read your CV before you submit it, ideally someone outside your own head who will actually tell you if a sentence is confusing. Confirm every phone number and email address on the document is one you currently check regularly, missed interview calls because of an old number is a genuinely common and entirely avoidable way to lose an opportunity. And save your final CV as a clearly named PDF, not an editable Word document with a generic filename like "CV 2" that gets lost in an HR inbox among fifty identically named files.

None of this requires expensive design software or a professional CV writer. It requires treating your CV as a document built for a busy, skimming reader, not an autobiography. Get that right, and a genuinely qualified candidate stops losing opportunities to a messier CV from someone less prepared.
